# Cold outreach

## Overview

A first message earns a reply when it proves you actually looked, asks for one small thing, and is easy to ignore politely. Almost every failing cold email fails on the first of those.

The specific way AI-written outreach fails is invented familiarity. "I saw you're scaling fast" when nothing said that. "Congrats on the new round" when there was no round. "Loved your post" when there was no post. Every one of those is a claim about the reader's own life that the reader can check instantly, and getting it wrong costs the account, not just the reply.

So the rule that governs this skill: every factual claim about them traces to a source with a date, or it does not go in the message.

## When to Use

- Writing a first email or LinkedIn message to someone who has not engaged before.
- Turning research or a trigger into an opener.
- Building or fixing a follow-up sequence.
- Someone pastes a name, a company or a profile and asks for something to send.
- An existing template gets no replies and needs diagnosing.

**Not for:** messaging existing customers, which has different rules and a different skill; replying to inbound interest, where you have their words to work from; drafting the research itself; or anything that needs volume automation on a platform whose terms forbid it.

## Work inside the team's setup

Four things change what you write, and none of them are guessable: where sends actually happen, which suppression list to check, who has to approve a message before it goes out, and which countries the recipients sit in, since that decides the rules that apply.

Establish them from what the team already has. Read their CRM if it is connected, read whatever doc holds their process, and ask for the rest in one short message. Do not guess any of the four, because the cost of guessing here lands on the whole team's deliverability rather than on this one message.

Then draft into the format the team already sends from, and do not propose a new tool.

## The gate

Before writing a word, check three things:

1. A dated, sourced fact exists that makes now different from any other week.
2. This person plausibly owns the problem, by role and scope rather than by title alone.
3. Nobody on the team already has an open thread with them.

Fail any of those and the answer is not a cleverer opener. Go back and research, pick a different contact, or drop the account. A message with nothing real in it costs more than sending nothing, because it burns the domain, the account and the team's name at once.

Volume is not the goal. Ten messages that clear the gate beat four hundred that do not.

## The Process

### 1. Pick the channel for where they actually are

Email suits a specific ask with a link or a document. A LinkedIn note is shorter, more informal, and gets read on a phone, so a paragraph is already too long. Match the norms of the channel, and follow the platform's rules on automation, connection requests and messaging limits.

### 2. Build the opener on one fact

One fact, paraphrased in your own words, with its date in your notes even when it is not in the text.

The test is simple: could this sentence be sent unchanged to another company in the same segment? If yes, it is filler and not an opener. "I saw you're growing" passes to anyone. "Your app added table-side payment in the July update" passes to nobody else.

Skip the flattery. Praise reads as automated even when sincere, because that is what automated mail does.

### 3. Say what you do in one line, in their language

One sentence, a specific outcome, and where possible a comparable they would recognise. Numbers if you have real ones. No superlatives, no category claims, nothing you could not defend on a call.

Never claim a prior conversation, a referral or a mutual connection you have not verified. It is the single fastest way to lose a deal, and in some places it is illegal.

### 4. One ask, small enough to answer in a word

Ask for the smallest thing that moves this forward. Often that is a question they can answer yes or no to, not a meeting. "Is multi-location reporting something you own, or does that sit with ops?" gets replies that "15 minutes to pick your brain" does not.

One ask only. Two asks read as a form.

### 5. Make it easy to ignore

A line that gives them a graceful exit raises replies rather than lowering them, and the replies you get are honest. "If this is not your area, happy to stop here" costs nothing.

### 6. Meet the compliance floor

Real name, real company, a real address where the law requires it, a working way to opt out, and the opt-out honoured on the first request. No fake reply chains, no "re:" on a thread that never existed, no forwarded-message theatre. Follow the rules that apply where the recipient sits, not where you sit.

Where consent rules apply to the recipient's country, note the lawful basis you are relying on, and keep the record of where their details came from.

### 7. Sequence: two or three follow-ups, each adding something, then stop

Every follow-up carries something new: a different angle, a relevant example, a shorter ask. None of them says "just bumping this" or "did you see my last email", which tells the reader their silence was already an answer and you ignored it.

Then stop, and record the stop. A sequence with no end is how a prospect list turns into a complaint list.

### 8. Run the fabrication check before sending

Read the draft and mark every factual claim. Each claim about them needs a source and a date. Each claim about you needs to be true. Anything unmarked comes out of the message, however good it sounded.

## Common Rationalizations

| Rationalization | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Personalisation at scale means merge fields." | A merge field proves a spreadsheet, not attention. The opener has to be a fact only this account has. |
| "I'll open with 'came across your profile'." | That phrase appears in every automated message ever sent. It signals the opposite of research. |
| "It's probably true that they're growing." | Probably true is invented. They can check it in a second, and being wrong about their own company ends the thread. |
| "A compliment warms it up." | Praise is what bulk mail does, so it reads as bulk mail. Specifics do the warming. |
| "More follow-ups lift reply rates." | They also lift complaints and spam reports, which cost the whole team's deliverability. Two or three, each with something new. |
| "Everyone fakes the re: line." | It is deceptive, it is unlawful in several places, and it is the first thing an annoyed recipient screenshots. |
| "I'll say a colleague suggested I reach out." | Not unless a colleague did. An unverifiable claim of a referral loses the account outright. |
| "Asking for the meeting is the whole point." | The meeting is what the reply leads to. A question they can answer in one word gets more meetings than a calendar link. |
| "The sequence says send today." | The sequence is not evidence that there is a reason to write. The gate is. |
| "It's a first touch, they won't check the details." | The details are about their own company. They are the one person who checks instantly. |
| "The AI wrote it, so the claim isn't really mine." | Your name is on it. Every claim in it is yours. |

## Red Flags

- Any factual claim about them with no source behind it.
- An opener that would work unchanged for another company in the segment.
- Flattery in the first two lines.
- More than one ask, or an ask that needs a calendar.
- Superlatives, category claims, or numbers you cannot source.
- A fake reply chain, a fake forward, or a subject line implying prior contact.
- A claimed referral or mutual connection nobody verified.
- No opt-out, or an opt-out that is not honoured immediately.
- Follow-ups that add nothing, or a sequence with no end.
- Sending because the sequence said so rather than because something changed.
- A message that would embarrass you if the recipient posted it publicly.

## Verification

- [ ] The gate was checked: dated fact, plausible owner, no open thread elsewhere
- [ ] The opener rests on one fact that fits this account and no other
- [ ] Every factual claim about them traces to a source with a date
- [ ] Every claim about you and your product is true and defensible
- [ ] There is exactly one ask, answerable without a calendar
- [ ] No flattery, no superlatives, no invented referral or prior contact
- [ ] The message offers a graceful way to say no
- [ ] Real identity, working opt-out, rules of the recipient's country followed
- [ ] No fake thread, forward, or subject line implying earlier contact
- [ ] Follow-ups each add something new, and the sequence has a stated end
- [ ] The fabrication check ran on the final text, not the first draft
