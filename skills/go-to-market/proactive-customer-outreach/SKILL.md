# Proactive customer outreach

## Overview

The blank page is the real problem here. A customer success or account manager knows a check-in is due, knows roughly what to say, and loses twenty minutes to the first sentence. Multiply that by forty accounts and the check-ins stop happening.

Filling the page badly costs more than leaving it empty. Two ways it goes wrong. The message reads like a template, which tells the customer they are on a list. Or it reads warmly and contains something untrue about their account, which tells them nobody is actually watching. The second is worse, and it is the one an eager draft produces.

What makes this work is that both the voice and the facts come from evidence: the voice from messages the team has already sent that got replies, the facts from the product and the CRM.

## When to Use

- An onboarding step has stalled and the customer needs a nudge.
- A milestone landed and someone should say so.
- Usage dropped, or an account went quiet.
- A renewal window is opening and the last real conversation is old.
- Something was promised on a call and is now due.
- Someone is staring at a blank page for any of the above.

**Not for:** cold outreach to people who are not customers; replying to an inbound message or a ticket, where their words are already in front of you; renewal negotiation; or the incident communication itself, meaning root-cause explanations, breach notifications, and any commitment you are not authorised to make.

Writing the account owner's honest status note is in scope. Inventing the status is not.

## Work inside the team's setup

Three things decide what this message looks like: where the team's best-performing messages live, what their support desk counts as an escalation, and where a send has to be logged so the next person sees it.

Read them out of whatever is connected, a process doc, the CRM, the support tool. Ask for whatever is left in one short message, because all three are one-line answers and all three change the output.

## Get the voice from evidence, not adjectives

Ask for three to five real messages the team has sent that worked, where worked means they got replies rather than that someone liked the writing. Reply rate is the signal. Well-written and unanswered is not the model to copy.

From those samples, pull out rules you can check against a draft:

typical length and sentence length, how they open, whether they use the person's first name, contractions or not, emoji or not, how the ask is phrased, the sign-off, and the things these messages never do. That last one carries the most weight, because it is what stops a draft drifting into generic customer-success voice.

The samples' rules travel to the new message. Their sentences do not. Reusing a phrase that worked once is how a team ends up with forty customers who have all received the same paragraph.

If nobody can supply top performers, use the last five messages actually sent and say plainly that the voice is current rather than best. If there are no samples at all, ask for one and draft in a plain, direct register meanwhile. Never take a description of the voice, "friendly but professional", as the input. That description is exactly what produces the robot voice everyone recognises.

## Get the account state from data, not impressions

Before drafting, establish from the product, the CRM, the tickets and the last call notes:

usage with numbers and dates rather than a direction, seats or licences in use against what they pay for, the milestone actually reached and when, open tickets and their severity, who spoke to them last and what got promised, and the renewal date.

**Sweep the whole account, not just the loudest thing on it.** List every open item before drafting: every ticket with its age and severity, every request made and not delivered, every onboarding step still incomplete. Then look at the oldest one, not the newest. A low-severity ticket that has sat for seventeen days is often a bigger relationship risk than the urgent one everybody is already watching, because nobody has told them it exists. Naming it unprompted is the single clearest signal that a human is actually looking at their account.

Any of those you cannot see is unknown, and unknowns stay out of the message. A cheerful "great to see the team getting so much value" to an account whose usage halved is worse than silence, and the customer knows which of you is looking at the data.

## The Process

### 1. Name the moment, with the observable behind it

Onboarding step incomplete after N days. Milestone reached on a date. Usage down a stated amount over a stated period. A promise made on a call and now due. Renewal in ninety days with no conversation since March.

If you cannot name the observable, there is no moment, and a message sent without one is the definition of noise.

### 2. Check the kill switch before writing

An open escalation, an unresolved high-severity ticket, an outage that hit them, a complaint in flight, a bad-news event at their company. In any of those cases a cheerful milestone note does damage that takes months to undo.

The switch stops the cheerful message, not all contact. Going quiet during someone's bad week is its own kind of damage, and it is the week they remember.

So when it trips, do three things. Name what must not go today, and hold it until the escalation closes. Then write the message that should go instead, which is almost always the honest one: lead with the thing that is actually happening, own anything promised and not delivered, commit to the next update rather than to a fix you cannot promise, and ask the one question that would speed the resolution up. Leave a blank only where nobody knows the answer yet, such as what engineering will say at midday, and never fill a blank with a guess.

During an incident, also offer a route that is faster than the queue they are already stuck in. A direct number, or a reply that reaches a person rather than a ticket. It costs nothing and it is the part they remember.

What you must not do is soften the check-in and send it anyway, or hand back a template of placeholders. A page of blanks is not a draft, it is the work pushed back to the person who asked.

### 3. Draft: voice rules, one real fact, one next step

Short. One account-specific fact with its number and date. One clear next step that is easy to say yes to or ignore. And if something was promised, open with that promise, because a customer remembers what they were told far better than anyone's CRM does.

### 4. Check the draft against the samples

Go line by line against the rules from step one. A sentence the samples would never contain gets rewritten, not defended as a preference. Length counts too: a message twice as long as the samples is off-voice even when every sentence is right.

### 5. Check the facts the same way

Every number in the draft traces to the tool it came from, with a date. Every claim about what they did needs to be a thing they did. Anything you inferred either comes out or gets rephrased as a question, which is often the better message anyway.

### 6. Log it, and list what the sender has to do today

The next person to touch this account needs to know what was said. An unlogged message is how a customer gets two nudges in one week from two people.

Then write down what the sender must actually do, today, for the message to be true. Every promise in it is a commitment somebody has to keep: an update by five, a report by Monday, a status chased out of engineering. A message that promises an update and is followed by silence does more damage than sending nothing, so the follow-through list is part of the deliverable rather than an afterthought.

## Common Rationalizations

| Rationalization | Reality |
|---|---|
| "It sounds fine to me." | Fine to you is not the team's voice. Check it against the samples, rule by rule. |
| "I'll write in a friendly, professional tone." | That description produces exactly the voice everyone recognises as automated. Voice comes from samples or not at all. |
| "I'll reuse the template that worked." | Its rules travel, its sentences do not. Forty customers with the same paragraph is the outcome. |
| "Usage is up, I'll mention that." | Numbers and dates, or leave it out. A vague claim about their data tells them you did not look. |
| "A milestone note is always welcome." | Not with an open severity-one ticket. Check the escalation state first. |
| "Hope you're well is a normal opener." | It is filler that marks the message as bulk. Open with the thing that made you write. |
| "I'll pick the best of these five samples." | The samples are evidence in aggregate. Pull the rules from all of them, then draft fresh. |
| "They'll appreciate hearing from us either way." | They will not. A message with no observable moment behind it teaches them to ignore the next one. |
| "The CRM is out of date, I'll go from memory." | Then the state is unknown. Say so, or check it. Memory is where invented account facts come from. |
| "I don't need to log it, it's just a check-in." | Two nudges in one week from two people, and the customer notices. |
| "There are no samples, I'll infer the voice from the website." | Marketing voice is not the voice a CSM writes in. Ask for one message and draft plainly meanwhile. |
| "I'll deal with the urgent ticket, that's what matters today." | The oldest neglected one is the relationship risk. Nobody is watching it, which is exactly why naming it lands. |
| "Keeping it short is the voice, so I'll drop the second debt." | Match the samples by default, and when there are two real debts say both and note the deviation. Silence on one of them is not brevity. |
| "The message promises an update, that's the sender's problem." | The promise is the deliverable. Write the follow-through list or the message makes things worse. |

## Red Flags

- A claim about their usage, seats or activity with no number and no date.
- A cheerful tone while an escalation or high-severity ticket is open.
- A message that would read identically for every customer on the list.
- Voice taken from a description of the voice rather than from real messages.
- Sentences lifted whole from a previous top performer.
- "Hope you're well", "just checking in", "circling back" as the opening move.
- No named observable behind the timing.
- No next step, or three of them.
- A promise from the last call left unaddressed.
- Length far outside what the samples run to.
- Nothing logged afterwards.
- Only the newest or loudest issue addressed, with older open items unmentioned.
- A promise made in the message with nothing recorded about who keeps it, and by when.
- An incident message that leaves them in the same queue with no faster route.

## Verification

- [ ] Three to five real messages were used as voice samples, chosen by reply rate
- [ ] Voice rules were extracted and the draft was checked against them line by line
- [ ] No sentence was lifted whole from a sample
- [ ] The moment names an observable with a number and a date
- [ ] Account state came from the product, CRM or tickets, not from memory
- [ ] The escalation check ran, and a tripped switch stopped the send
- [ ] Every number in the draft traces to a tool and a date
- [ ] Anything inferred was cut or turned into a question
- [ ] Any outstanding promise from the last conversation is addressed first
- [ ] Exactly one next step, easy to answer or ignore
- [ ] Where samples were unavailable, the output says whose voice it is written in
- [ ] Every open item on the account was listed before drafting, oldest included
- [ ] Any promise in the message has a matching follow-through action, with an owner and a time
- [ ] During an incident, a faster route than the ticket queue was offered
- [ ] The message is logged where the team keeps account history
